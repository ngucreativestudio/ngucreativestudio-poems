🌑 When Silence Began to Cry
​Opening Invocation:
​“The earth remembers what we forget, and in her silence, she still speaks to those who listen.”
​Only the patient can hear what silence is saying.
​We were not asleep, only wandering between dawn and memory. The song rose softly — “Ide writing na ato m uto…” Our tongues answered with joy, our laughter spelling the word as if it were prayer. Once, we knew what togetherness felt like — children carrying firewood in rhythm, hands washing in the same stream, stories told under moonlight that made us believe again. We were many, yet we breathed as one.
​Then one morning, as the mist bowed to the sun, we looked around — and the laughter had gone quiet. Where are the voices that once gathered us? Who broke the circle? Who took the gourd of unity and spilled its content on the ground?
​We cried, not because the stories ended, but because no one remained to tell them. We asked the wind, agu gini meme? The wind replied in silence. And that silence — it began to bleed.
​Our today will be classified as something strange — a people who saw nothing, yet their eyes wept blood. Is the mother angry too? What a rage of the unseen.
​They said, “These were not the first,” and mentioned names of those who spoke truth. Who killed, and who was killed? We stood afraid — not of death, but of forgetting.
​And in that stillness, a softer voice rises within us, carrying a truth older than fear:
​Are we afraid of death? Should we truly fear it — when death itself is but a call to return home?
So tell me, are you afraid of going home?
​I remember when there was joy in farming — a sacred form of gratitude to the Mother Earth. We ate what she gave us with reverence, but now the foods that once preserved life are feared, as though they bring death.
​We once prayed and offered to the earth for rain, but today, when a drop falls, many grow angry instead of thankful — and the tears that fall are heavier than my own.
​Who will help? Are you leaving the fight for me alone? I cannot do this alone — I still need you. Can’t you see that our culture is fading, slowly, like smoke carried by the wind?
​Some say this song is old, that it belongs to another time — yet it flows through me, alive, and speaks to this generation.
​A mother asked: “Are you a spirit? Why do you sound like one?”
A friend said: “This song is of old — are you of this generation?”
​Perhaps this is the voice of memory, a spirit of the ancestors calling through me, so that the living may remember.
​Do you wish the history of the Igbo people to end — forgetting that we were once called the mother of all languages?
​Now even truth walks barefoot, for no one dares to clothe it. And those who know keep silent, as if silence itself has sworn them to secrecy.
​But remember — silence too can cry, and when it does, the earth will answer.
